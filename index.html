/02

How itworks

  1. Average the forehead

    Every frame, the page averages the red, green and blue values inside the forehead box. Blood volume changes with each heartbeat and shifts skin color by a fraction of a percent.

  2. Separate pulse from light

    The color traces are resampled to 30 samples per second and combined with the POS algorithm (Wang et al., 2017), which keeps the pulse and cancels most brightness changes.

  3. Find the beat

    The signal is filtered and its spectrum searched between 42 and 210 beats per minute. The strongest peak becomes the heart rate.

  4. Score the trust

    Three checks feed a 0–100 score: how clearly the pulse peak stands out from noise, how still the forehead is, and whether the light is too dark or overexposed.

  5. Refuse to bluff

    Below 40, the number is crossed out. A confident wrong reading is worse than no reading.

/03

The buildlog

Teaching a webcam to say when it’s wrong.

In my Learn article, “What’s stopping your webcam from becoming a health sensor?”, I looked at why rPPG still isn’t trusted outside the lab. The pulse signal hiding in your skin is tiny, and movement, bad lighting and video compression can bury it completely. Yet most rPPG demos show a heart rate no matter how bad the video is. That felt like the real gap to me: a wrong number that looks confident is more dangerous than no number at all.

So for my build, I made a browser-based pulse check that does two things. It estimates heart rate from the forehead using the POS algorithm, and it gives every reading a quality score from 0 to 100 based on pulse strength, movement and lighting. When the score drops, it tells you why.

How I tested it

I don’t own a smartwatch, so I used the oldest heart-rate sensor there is: two fingers on my wrist. While the camera ran, I counted my pulse for 30 seconds, doubled it, and compared that to the webcam’s reading. I tested four conditions: sitting still, talking, moving my head and dim light. Across all of them, the webcam stayed within about 2–3 bpm of my own count.

What I learned

The surprise wasn’t the heart rate, it was the trust score. Even when the webcam matched my count, my sitting-still readings only scored between 47 and 55, in the “use with caution” zone, because the pulse signal from my forehead was weak. So right now the score is too careful: it doubts readings that turn out to be correct. The heart rate itself also moved between 60 and 70 bpm across takes while I sat still, which shows how small and noisy this signal really is. My reference wasn’t perfect either, since missing a single beat in a 30-second count shifts the result by 2 bpm.

Why this matters for Hemio

Hemio is built on health data people can trust. Before a camera-based measurement can ever sit next to a blood test result, it has to know when it’s unreliable. This quality layer is my first piece of the rPPG data pipeline I want to build into the Hemio app.

What’s next

Calibrate the quality score so it matches the real error instead of being overly cautious, repeat the test with a clip-on pulse oximeter as a more precise reference, replace the fixed forehead box with face tracking, and test across more skin tones and lighting setups.

This is a research prototype, not a medical device. Don’t use it to make health decisions.
