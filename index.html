Who thisis for

Operators of humanoid and teleoperated robots in safety-critical settings: remote surgery, hazardous-environment work, logistics.

The failure it prevents: a tired or distracted operator making a control error while a physical robot acts on the other end.

Sensing operator state in real time is a prerequisite for trusting a human-in-the-loop robot. The camera is already there, so the sensor costs nothing extra and the operator wears nothing.

/03

How itworks

  1. Find the skin

    Face tracking finds your forehead and both cheeks in every frame, and the page averages the red, green and blue values inside each area. Blood volume changes with each heartbeat and shifts skin color by a fraction of a percent. If tracking can’t start or can’t find your face, a fixed box on the forehead takes over.

  2. Separate pulse from light

    The color traces are resampled to 30 samples per second and combined with the POS algorithm (Wang et al., 2017), which keeps the pulse and cancels most brightness changes.

  3. Find the beat

    The signal is filtered and its spectrum searched between 42 and 210 beats per minute. The areas with the clearest pulse count the most, and the strongest peak becomes the heart rate.

  4. Score the trust

    Three checks feed a 0–100 score: how clearly the pulse peak stands out from noise, how still your face is, and whether the light is too dark or overexposed. This score only says whether the measurement is reliable. It says nothing about the operator.

  5. Refuse to bluff

    Below 40, the number is crossed out. A confident wrong reading is worse than no reading.

  6. Judge the operator, separately

    Once the signal is good, the page asks you to hold still for 10 seconds and collects every trustworthy estimate. If they agree, the median becomes the locked heart rate. If they don’t, it asks you to try again. The operator state is judged once, from that locked reading and how much the heart rate moved during the hold. Ready means 50 to 90 bpm and steady. Caution means 90 to 110 bpm, a less steady rate, or a reading that is only just reliable. Not recommended means over 110 or under 45 bpm, or a rate that swings a lot. These thresholds are placeholders until they are calibrated.

  7. Know what heart rate can’t tell you

    Heart rate and its variability are one input to operator readiness, not the whole picture. True fatigue and stress detection needs heart-rate variability (HRV) and per-person calibration. That calibration is exactly where biomarker data from Hemio fits, turning a generic signal into a per-operator baseline.

/04

The buildlog

Teaching a webcam to say when it’s wrong.

In my Learn article, “What’s stopping your webcam from becoming a health sensor?”, I looked at why rPPG still isn’t trusted outside the lab. The pulse signal hiding in your skin is tiny, and movement, bad lighting and video compression can bury it completely. Yet most rPPG demos show a heart rate no matter how bad the video is. That felt like the real gap to me: a wrong number that looks confident is more dangerous than no number at all.

So for my build, I made a browser-based pulse check that does two things. It estimates heart rate from the forehead using the POS algorithm, and it gives every reading a quality score from 0 to 100 based on pulse strength, movement and lighting. When the score drops, it tells you why.

How I tested it

I don’t own a smartwatch, so I used the oldest heart-rate sensor there is: two fingers on my wrist. While the camera ran, I counted my pulse for 30 seconds, doubled it, and compared that to the webcam’s reading. I tested four conditions: sitting still, talking, moving my head and dim light. Across all of them, the webcam stayed within about 2–3 bpm of my own count.

What I learned

The surprise wasn’t the heart rate, it was the trust score. Even when the webcam matched my count, my sitting-still readings only scored between 47 and 55, in the “use with caution” zone, because the pulse signal from my forehead was weak. So right now the score is too careful: it doubts readings that turn out to be correct. The heart rate itself also moved between 60 and 70 bpm across takes while I sat still, which shows how small and noisy this signal really is. My reference wasn’t perfect either, since missing a single beat in a 30-second count shifts the result by 2 bpm.

Why this matters for Hemio

Hemio is built on health data people can trust. Before a camera-based measurement can ever sit next to a blood test result, it has to know when it’s unreliable. This quality layer is my first piece of the rPPG data pipeline I want to build into the Hemio app.

What’s next

Calibrate the quality score so it matches the real error instead of being overly cautious, repeat the test with a clip-on pulse oximeter as a more precise reference, replace the fixed forehead box with face tracking, and test across more skin tones and lighting setups. For the operator-state layer: HRV-based fatigue detection, per-operator calibration with Hemio biomarker data, and validation against operator-load scenarios, not just rest.

This is a research prototype, not a medical device. Don’t use it to make health decisions.
